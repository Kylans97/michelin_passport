 MICHELIN Guide Announces Its First Tianjin Restaurant Selection in China, with Beijing and Tianjin Selections Released Together
 The MICHELIN Guide arrives in Tianjin, where a centuries-old port-city culture has produced a distinctive cuisine — from iconic jianbing guozi to a new generation of restaurants rethinking local flavors.

 By the end of October 2026, the MICHELIN Guide arrives in Tianjin for the first time, unveiling its first Tianjin restaurant selection together with the Beijing restaurant selection as a combined Beijing & Tianjin release. The launch event will take place in Tianjin, bringing international attention to one of northern China’s most distinctive food cities.Where the Haihe River meets the Bohai Sea, Tianjin has been a gateway for trade and migration for centuries. Its cuisine bears the imprint of that history, but it is not simply a story of outside influences. Known locally as “Jinmen”, Tianjin cooking has developed its own character, from hearty traditional dishes to a growing contemporary cooking scene rooted in local ingredients, techniques and an international.
 “Our expansion across China follows a clear and deliberate strategic thread: we move region by region, not by chance, but by the richness of each local culinary identity. Every new destination is chosen because its food culture tells a story — shaped by history, geography, and the people who cook and serve with pride. Tianjin is a perfect embodiment of this approach. Its centuries-old canal and maritime heritage have forged a ‘Jinmen’ flavor that is unlike any other — a bold, layered, and deeply authentic gastronomy that deserves to be celebrated on the global stage. Adding Tianjin to our selection is not an isolated decision; it is the natural next step in our ongoing mission to map the extraordinary diversity of Chinese cuisine, one remarkable region at a time.” — Gwendal Poullennec, International Director of the MICHELIN Guide 
 The Tianjin skyline. © Tianjin Municipal Bureau of Culture and Tourism
 A City That Takes Breakfast SeriouslyIf you want to understand Tianjin’s food culture, start early.In Northwest Corner, breakfast is a neighborhood ritual. More than 20 local specialties are served across its busy streets, but the one most closely associated with Tianjin is jianbing guozi. The crepe is made with mung-bean batter, topped with crispy fried dough and sauces, then folded up to order. It is simple, filling and fiercely local.There is mahua, too — crisp, twisted fried dough, sometimes made with layered fillings — and fried cake, crunchy on the outside and soft and sweet within. Traditional dishes such as zengbeng liyu, deep-fried carp presented as though leaping from the plate, and bazhen tofu, tofu served with eight delicacies, show another side of the city’s cooking.But Tianjin’s food scene is not frozen in tradition. Chefs are looking again at the ingredients and techniques that have defined local cooking, bringing them into contemporary restaurants and asking what Jinmen cuisine can look like today.That gives Tianjin an interesting tension: its most characteristic food is still found on the street, while a more ambitious restaurant scene is beginning to reinterpret it.
 A view of Tianjin City at night. © Tianjin Municipal Bureau of Culture and Tourism
 Beyond the BowlTianjin’s food makes more sense once you understand the city around it.The Haihe River cuts through the center of Tianjin before reaching the Bohai Sea. At night, historic buildings along the waterfront are reflected in the water, and the river becomes one of the best places to see the city.The Five Great Avenues tell a different part of the story. More than 2,000 villas and residences line the streets here, many built in British, French and Italian architectural styles. Together, they offer a striking reminder of the international communities that once made Tianjin home.At the Ancient Culture Street, more than 600 years of local traditions survive in crafts such as Yangliuqing New Year paintings and clay figurines. In teahouses, visitors can also encounter crosstalk, Tianjin’s quick-fire tradition of comic dialogue.The city’s more recent history is visible in former residences of modern luminaries, while the Tianjin Eye, a Ferris wheel built over the Haihe River, provides a bird’s-eye view of the city.The first MICHELIN Guide Tianjin restaurant selection puts the city’s restaurants on the international map — from the breakfast stalls that have fed generations of locals to restaurants now finding new ways to tell Tianjin’s culinary story.
